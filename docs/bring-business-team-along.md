It's important to recognize and acknowledge the lead and two agents as domain experts.
We'd start by establishing a baseline to compare the new system against, reviewing the last 100 customer support tickets and labeling them. Whether they were resolved or not resolved, whether they could have been automated, and whether they should never be automated. Their instincts become written policy.
Then we'd determine the metrics that serve as trust signals for the system — false-resolve rate, missed escalations, and human decision agreement on holdout sets. Domain experts set thresholds, with my guidance.
No system enters the wild with immediate trust. I'd suggest a phased rollout, never moving to the next level of exposure until the previous trust criteria are met. First, a shadow phase where decisions are logged and scored against the rubric but never reach the member. Then a phase allowing agent edits and routing with explicit HITL approval. Then we continue widening capabilities and allowances as system integrity grows.
To facilitate handoff, I'd identify a champion to share success stories and tips and help drive adoption within the department. I'd also name a system owner and work with them to define a living playbook for maintenance and for feeding bad resolution cases back into the evaluation set. Regular check-ins about their findings, adoption rate, and perceived problems during the phased rollouts will catch issues early and build trust.
Throughout the process, we must validate the skeptical agent, because their concern is legitimate: AI handling of customer-facing data and responses CAN be fraught with issues like negative customer sentiment and compliance landmines. But their skepticism and domain knowledge can be leveraged to identify failure modes early. I'd make them the owner of the evaluation set — giving them authority over the exact mechanism that mitigates their concerns. And if the skepticism originates in fear of role replacement, I'd address that directly. The system doesn't replace human judgment; it reserves it for the higher-leverage 20%.
